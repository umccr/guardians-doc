<div class="satre-changed">
  <div class="satre-title">
    <span>🔶</span> 1.4.01 — Changed for Genomics
  </div>
  <p><strong>SATRE (Mandatory):</strong> You must have checks to ensure a project has legal, financial and ethical requirements in place for the duration of the project.</p>
  <p class="satre-context mb-2">Standard requirement for project governance checks.</p>
  <p><strong>Genomics context:</strong> Genomics adds per-participant consent scope complexity. A dataset may not be homogeneously consented — individual objects may or may not be releasable to a given project depending on the original consent scope. The concept of a "cohort" belonging to one patient but of interest to multiple studies, institutions, and investigators means privacy and ethical boundaries must be managed per-participant, not just per-project. DAC processes and DSA templates often still assume a release model — ethics committees don't consistently recognise TRE access as a distinct (lower-risk) mode.</p>
</div>

<div class="satre-changed">
  <div class="satre-title">
    <span>🔶</span> 1.4.04 — Changed for Genomics
  </div>
  <p><strong>SATRE (Mandatory):</strong> You must have standard processes for end of project, following legal requirements and data security best practice.</p>
  <p class="satre-context mb-2">Includes archiving of quality/log data and archiving or deletion of datasets.</p>
  <p><strong>Genomics context:</strong> End-of-project processes for genomics are complicated by data scale (100s of TB cannot be casually "archived") and the potential for re-use. Derived data (variant calls, annotations) may have value beyond the original project. The TRE must distinguish between: (a) revoking researcher access, (b) retaining data for future authorised projects under existing custodian agreements, and (c) genuine deletion where consent requires it. Storage costs for large genomics datasets make indefinite retention expensive — clear policies on what is retained vs deleted are essential.</p>
</div>
