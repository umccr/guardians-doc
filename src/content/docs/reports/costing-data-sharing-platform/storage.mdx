Storage cost is what you pay to keep analysis‑ready genomics data at rest in S3 buckets, 
month after month. Unlike standing cost (which is fixed regardless of data volume),
 storage cost grows with how much data you keep and how long you keep it.

In a data‑sharing platform, storage decisions have a big impact on long‑term sustainability. You must choose:

- **How much to keep**: do you archive everything, or only curated outputs?
- **How long to keep it**: do you retain data indefinitely, or delete after a certain period?
- **Where to keep it**: do you use fast, expensive storage (S3 Standard) or slower,
 cheaper tiers (S3 Glacier, S3 Deep Archive)?

These choices compound over time. A platform that keeps everything hot forever will see storage cost grow linearly 
over time, while one with thoughtful archiving and deletion policies can keep costs much flatter.

Here we will describe the cost of different storage classes and data volumes. 
The strategies on managing storage cost over time through lifecycle policies are addressed in [Looking after data](../data-lifecycle/).

## Main cost components

At a high level, storage cost breaks down into a few main contributors:


### S3 buckets and storage classes

**S3 is the core storage layer** for analysis‑ready data in a data‑sharing platform. All datasets, results, 
and platform metadata are stored in S3 buckets. S3 pricing has two main dimensions:

- **Volume of data stored** (per GB per month) – the more data you keep, the more you pay.
- **Storage class** (how the data is stored and how quickly it can be retrieved) – this is the biggest cost lever.


**Storage classes** let you trade cost for access speed and retrieval flexibility. The main S3 classes are:

- **Standard** – millisecond access, highest cost. Use for frequently accessed data.
- **Standard-IA (Infrequent Access)** – millisecond access, lower cost than Standard, 
but you pay per retrieval and have a 30-day minimum storage duration. Use for data accessed occasionally.
- **Glacier Instant Retrieval** – millisecond access, significantly cheaper than Standard, 
no retrieval charges, but 90-day minimum storage duration. Use for archive data that you rarely access but might need immediately.
- **Glacier Flexible Retrieval** – retrieval takes 1–5 minutes (Standard option) to 12+ hours (Bulk option), very low cost, 90-day minimum storage duration. Use for rarely accessed long-term data where retrieval speed is not critical.
- **Glacier Deep Archive** – retrieval takes 12+ hours, lowest cost, 180-day minimum storage duration. 
Use for long-term compliance or backup where you almost never need to retrieve data.


### Metadata and indexing

Beyond raw data storage, platforms need to track and index information *about* the data. This includes dataset names, ownership, creation dates, access history, and other operational metadata. These metadata systems are essential for users to discover, understand, and access datasets, and also consume storage space.

Platforms typically maintain three types of metadata storage:

- **S3 object metadata** (tags, versions, access logs) – small but persistent. AWS stores this alongside your data in S3.
- **Metadata databases** (e.g. [DynamoDB](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/amazondynamodb/latest/developerguide/Introduction.html) or [RDS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonRDS/latest/UserGuide/Welcome.html) tables) that index datasets, track provenance, and record access history. These enable structured queries and operational tracking.
- **Catalogue and search indices** (e.g. OpenSearch or similar search service) that allow users to discover datasets, enabling full-text search and exploration of available data.

These systems are usually much smaller than the raw data itself, but they accumulate over time and contribute a steady storage footprint to the platform's overall cost.



### Replication and multi‑region storage

Data-sharing platforms often need to replicate data for two reasons: **resilience** (so data survives infrastructure failures) and **compliance** (to meet jurisdictional or regulatory requirements). Replication means keeping copies of data in multiple locations, which multiplies storage cost.

Platforms typically replicate data in two ways:

- **Across regions** – keeping copies in different AWS regions. This protects against regional outages and meets data residency requirements (e.g. "data must be stored in Australia"). Each additional region doubles the storage cost for that data.
- **Across accounts** – keeping copies in different AWS accounts (e.g. one for your organisation, one for a partner). This allows partners to access data within their own AWS environment and provides isolation for governance and billing. Each additional account adds storage cost.

Replication costs scale quickly. A dataset kept in two regions costs roughly twice as much at rest, even before considering the transfer charges for keeping the replicas in sync.






## Internal vs cross‑organisation platforms: different storage growth patterns

Storage needs and growth patterns differ significantly between**internal sharing platform (ISP)** and a **cross‑organisation sharing platform (CSP)**. 
While both use the same storage components, how data accumulates and is managed over time varies considerably.


### Internal Sharing Platform

A ISP will be characterised by **centralised, single-region storage** with
 simpler replication needs. Data is stored and managed within a single organisation's AWS environment:

- Within the organisation, each project produces and stores its own sequencing and analysis results 
(e.g. FASTQs, aligned BAMs, VCFs, summary tables).
- Data for **active projects** is kept in a hot tier (e.g. S3 Standard) to support fast access.
- Old projects' data may be archived to Glacier or deleted after a retention period.
- Metadata, catalogues and logs are centralised in one account, usually in one region.

**Typical storage growth**: Assuming projects of similar size, total storage grows roughly linearly with the 
number of projects and time. 

**Storage cost shape**: relatively predictable. If you keep everything hot, cost grows steadily.
If you archive after 1–2 years and delete intermediates, cost can plateau or grow only slowly.



### Cross‑Organisation Sharing Platform (CSP)

A CSP will be characterised by **distributed, multi-region or multi-account storage** with complex replication 
and governance requirements. Data must be accessible to external partners while meeting compliance and resilience standards:

- **External access requirements**: data must be available to external partners, not just the originating team. 
This often requires replication across regions or accounts for resilience, compliance, and performance.
- **Multiple copies of data**: partners may request their own copies of datasets, creating redundant storage of 
the same data across different locations or accounts.
- **Stricter governance**: audit and compliance requirements mandate longer retention periods, additional catalogues,
and detailed access logs. Multi‑account architectures spread metadata and logs across several accounts.


**Typical storage growth**: faster and less predictable than ISP. Partner requests for dataset copies, replication 
across regions and accounts, and compliance-driven retention all contribute to rapid growth. Storage can accumulate 
quickly even if per-project output volumes are similar to an ISP.

**Storage cost shape**: more volatile than ISP. Sudden partner requests, new compliance rules, or additional replication 
can create cost spikes. Strict lifecycle policies and careful replication management are essential to keep costs under 
control. See [Looking after data](../data-lifecycle/).




## Design choices

There is significant room for design decisions in storage. Storage cost depends on several key architectural choices: 
selecting storage classes that fit your access patterns, deciding what data to keep and for how long, and automating 
those decisions. These choices will determine whether storage cost grows sustainably or spirals out of control.




### What to keep: curated outputs vs "keep everything"

A key choice is **which artefacts to keep**. This decision shapes both your storage footprint and your ability to reproduce or re-run analyses.

**Keep everything**: store raw inputs, scratch files, intermediate outputs and final results.
- Pros: maximum reproducibility; easier to re‑run analyses from any point.
- Cons: storage cost grows quickly; most intermediates are rarely accessed after initial validation.

**Curated outputs only**: store only validated outputs (e.g. final BAM/CRAM, VCFs, summary tables); discard intermediates once accepted.
- Pros: much smaller footprint; easier to manage and audit; significantly lower cost.
- Cons: complex re‑runs may require regenerating intermediates from raw data.

The choice depends on your platform's priorities: how much reproducibility you need versus how aggressively you want to control costs, and whether raw sequencing data and scratch storage are managed separately from your data-sharing layer.



### Where to keep it: storage class selection

Another choice is **which storage class to use for different types of data**. This decision depends on 
understanding your platform's access patterns and data characteristics.

You have several options across the spectrum:

**Frequently accessed data**: S3 Standard (millisecond access, highest cost) or S3 Standard-IA (millisecond access, 
lower cost, but per-retrieval charges and 30-day minimum).

**Archive data with occasional access**: S3 Glacier Instant Retrieval (millisecond 
access if needed, significantly cheaper than Standard, 90-day minimum) or S3 Glacier Flexible Retrieval 
(retrieval takes 1–5 minutes to 12+ hours depending on tier, very low cost, 90-day minimum).

**Long-term compliance or backup**: S3 Glacier Deep Archive (retrieval takes 12+ hours, lowest cost, 180-day minimum).

**Making the choice**: understand your platform's usage patterns:
- Which datasets are queried regularly by users?
- Which are accessed occasionally for validation or re-analysis?
- Which are kept primarily for compliance or audit trails?
- How quickly do different datasets need to be retrieved?

Different datasets warrant different storage classes. A frequently shared reference dataset 
might use Standard, while validated results from completed projects might use Glacier Instant, and 
compliance archives might use Deep Archive.

Also consider that as datasets age, their access patterns may change, moving data between storage classes can
 optimise cost. This is covered in detail in [Looking after data](../data-lifecycle/).


### Replication strategy

Multi‑region or multi‑account replication serves important purposes: ensuring datasets survive regional outages, 
meeting jurisdictional data residency requirements, or serving geographically distributed users. Both ISPs and CSPs 
may use replication, though CSPs often need it for external partner access and regulatory compliance.

The cost of replication is significant: each additional region or account roughly doubles the at-rest storage cost 
for replicated data, and introduces ongoing expenses for keeping copies in sync. 

The design challenge is deciding which datasets justify replication. For ISPs, replication might be limited to 
critical datasets or backups. For CSPs, replication is often essential for partner access and compliance, making it 
a necessary cost of "doing business".

### Assumptions and estimates

Below, we describe representative setups we assumed for an ISP and a CSP, and the corresponding estimates for each component.

#### Active curated outputs (hot storage)

**ISP**

- Curated outputs for around 50 projects stored in S3 Standard.
- Approximate volume: 5–10 TB of BAM/CRAM, VCFs and summary tables.
- Typical monthly cost: **\$100–300** for hot storage of these TB‑scale curated outputs.

**CSP**

- Similar curated outputs, but a larger active set due to ongoing collaborative work.
- Approximate volume: 10–20 TB of datasets kept hot to support partners.
- Typical monthly cost: **\$200–600**, reflecting higher TB volumes kept in hot storage.

AWS pricing references:  
[S3 storage classes](https://aws.amazon.com/s3/pricing/).

#### Archived outputs (cold storage)

**ISP**

- Older curated outputs moved to Glacier or Deep Archive after a defined period (e.g. 12–24 months).
- Approximate volume: 10–30 TB of archived results across a few years of projects.
- Typical monthly cost: **\$50–150** for archival storage in Glacier classes.

**CSP**

- Larger archival footprint due to longer retention for cross‑organisational projects.
- Approximate volume: 20–60 TB of archived datasets, possibly across multiple regions or accounts.
- Typical monthly cost: **\$100–300**, depending on tier mix and retention length.

AWS pricing references:  
[S3 Glacier & Deep Archive](https://aws.amazon.com/s3/pricing/).

#### Metadata and indexing

**ISP**

- A small number of metadata tables (e.g. DynamoDB or RDS) indexing datasets, projects and permissions.
- Overall size is modest compared to raw data (GB‑scale rather than TB‑scale).
- Typical monthly cost: **\$20–50** for core metadata stores.

**CSP**

- More metadata tables and indexes, spread across multiple accounts and possibly regions.
- Additional catalogues to support partner‑specific views of datasets.
- Typical monthly cost: **\$50–100** for distributed metadata stores.

AWS pricing references:  
[DynamoDB](https://aws.amazon.com/dynamodb/pricing/), [RDS](https://aws.amazon.com/rds/pricing/).

#### Replication and multi‑region copies

**ISP**

- Primarily single‑region storage; little or no cross‑region replication.
- Occasional copies for backup or special projects, but not routine.
- Typical monthly cost: **\$0–50** for replication‑related storage.

**CSP**

- Routine replication of key datasets across regions or accounts for resilience and data residency.
- Partners may retain their own copies in their accounts.
- Typical monthly cost: **\$100–300** attributable to replicated copies and partner‑side storage.

AWS pricing references:  
[S3 replication and multi‑region storage](https://aws.amazon.com/s3/pricing/).
