## Summary

The GUARDIANS TRE Working Group has spent 2026 investigating trusted research environments (TRE) for genomics. During
this time, it reviewed the UK [SATRE specification](https://satre-specification.readthedocs.io/en/v2.0.1/) requirements,
asking whether genomics differentiates itself enough that TRE operators and builders will need to
build in specific genomics functionality.
This report outlines the findings and compares SATRE requirements in the [SATRE for Genomics](../../requirements/satre-genomics/) page.

The group's main finding is that SATRE is a good base for a genomics TRE. SATRE leaves each implementor to decide how to
meet the spec, and has been written with multiple use-cases in mind. This means that the majority of its requirements
apply unchanged. Genomic data does however sharpen familiar risks, some of which are considerations about identifiable,
large scale data, and how compute and storage influences that. The working group noted that other health data carries
similar risks, and is already used by implementors of SATRE. SATRE itself is therefore deemed generally fit for genomics
data. However, there are still some key differences, and this report contains recommendations for applying SATRE to
genomics, some of which could be fed back to SATRE.

Where genomics differs, the reason is usually scale or how quickly demand changes. SATRE grew out of TREs for health
records, where a project is typically a virtual desktop at a known price point, holding small amounts of tabular data. A
genomics project may read hundreds of terabytes, start and stop hundreds of machines, and spend thousands of dollars
within days. This can all happen for a burst period of a few days, and then stop for months. That puts a few topics at
the centre of a genomics TRE: scalable compute, workflow management, storage and data lifecycle, cost, and egress.

Throughout meetings, the group used the following tests:

- A **note** keeps the SATRE line. In this case genomics makes it harder to meet or has a particular edge case that
  isn't well considered in other fields. The majority of points are notes.
- A **change or new requirement** applies where genomics needs the TRE to build or run something it otherwise would not.
- **Feedback** covers concerns with SATRE lines that are not specific to genomics, recorded so they can be raised with
  SATRE.

Some of these needs are shared with medical imaging and AI research, and genomics is not wholly unique in its
requirements. It was decided to record what genomics needs, even though that may be a common need in another field too.
The needs that are truly unique to genomics would be the ones that need extra emphasis to vendors or TRE operators, as
they may not have been considered in other fields. Needs that aren't covered by SATRE but do appear in other fields that
aren't just genomics also deserve a mention, and more work needs to be done to communicate with other fields and learn
from their TRE usecases.

## Group methodology

The group has met monthly since April 2026. Early meetings covered definitions of a TRE as members understood it and the
platforms they had used. From August, the group began reviewing pillars 1 to 4 of SATRE v2.0.1, setting aside [pillar 5
on federation](https://satre-specification.readthedocs.io/en/v2.0.1/specification.html#federation) for now. The chair
prepared a review document for each pillar, which proposed possible genomics changes from earlier discussions. Members
then decided in the meeting whether each draft was a note, a change, or not needed. Decisions were made by consensus of
the members present, and attendance ranged from about seven to twenty people.

This report uses three terms for the status of a point:

- **Agreed** means a meeting decided it.
- **Open** means a meeting discussed it but did not decide.
- **Proposed** means it comes from a review document, and the group has not discussed it yet.

The review is not finished, and this report is a working draft for members to comment on.

## Operators provide tools, custodians decide

A common principle was established by the group which shaped answers. Generally speaking, the group operated under the
principle that the TRE operator provides the tools that carry out governance decisions, and the data custodian, or the
institutions accountable for the research, make decisions. In data protection terms, the operator is a data processor,
not a data controller. The principle is not unique to genomics, but it comes up often, because consent can differ for
each participant.

The group applied it to:

- **Publishing project details.** This should be the custodian's decision, because some custodians cannot publish, for
  example for Indigenous data. The group plans to raise this with SATRE.
- **Consent.** The custodian resolves differences in consent before data reaches a project. Withdrawal belongs to cohort
  construction, and usually takes effect at the next data release.
- **Ethics.** SATRE's data lifecycle lines mention legal and regulatory implications, but not ethics. Members did not
  want the operator to judge whether research is ethical. But the TRE must carry out the conditions that ethics
  approvals set, such as when data must be deleted.
- **How strict to be.** The custodian chooses how open access to outside software is, and the custodian or operator
  chooses what happens at a budget limit.

The group also agreed to add notes where SATRE refers to UK law and UK bodies. Data sovereignty matters too, because
some data must stay in Australia.

## Scalable compute

**What SATRE assumes.** SATRE [2.1.15] recommends access to HPC or other scalable compute if users need it.

**What differs for genomics.** A pipeline run requires differing compute as it changes needs while running. It might use
16 CPUs with little memory for some time, then one CPU with 128 GiB of memory, or many small machines. Across a cohort,
hundreds of machines can be busy at once, and they shouldn't cost anything while idle. The group also calls this
dynamic compute. Members saw
it as one of the main things that separates a genomics TRE from an ordinary one. Experience also confirmed this, where
for example some commercial TREs can start many machines at once, but not scale automatically, and some TREs do not
support Spark clusters.

Three forms of dynamic compute were discussed:

- **Automatic scaling.** A workflow engine creates machines for each stage and removes them afterwards.
- **A cluster on demand.** A researcher starts a cluster, such as a Spark cluster, and shuts it down afterwards.
- **A machine by the hour.** A researcher hires a GPU or a large machine for a few hours.

Researchers care less about how the compute is provided than about getting it easily from inside the TRE. A TRE must
also allow the ability to remove compute and data after finishing, as this can be an easy step to forget. All these
factors in relation to scalable compute were a common theme in the group on what constitutes a difference between a
genomics TRE and other fields.

**What was decided.**

- Agreed: the requirements must describe dynamic compute as a capability, give the three forms as examples, and include
  clean-up.
- Open: The review document proposes a new Mandatory requirement, however it's not clear whether the existing [2.1.15]
  could be made Mandatory instead, or whether recommended would be enough. Either way, clear description is important
  for this requirement.

## Workflow management

**What SATRE assumes.** SATRE requires a TRE to provide the software its users need ([2.1.10]), and mentions pipelines
only as a reproducibility practice ([2.1.11]). Access to container registries is optional ([2.1.12]), and outbound
internet access is blocked by default ([2.2.11]). A request for SATRE to add guidance on containerised workflows was
closed until an established pattern exists ([#344](https://github.com/sa-tre/satre-specification/issues/344)).

**What differs for genomics.** Genomics adds to this requirement because a workflow engine, such as Nextflow or CWL,
requires compute created for each stage to function properly. This is how a lot of genomics work happens, and it depends
on containers, genomics-specific tools and community pipelines. Often an out-of-date engine cannot run newer pipelines
and distributed analysis, such as a Spark cluster for Hail, needs infrastructure beyond a single workspace. The group
agreed that a researcher should not have to set up this kind of infrastructure themselves, and that the TRE operators
should do so.

Additionally, since a lot of genomics software comes from GitHub, Bioconda and container registries, provisions should
be in place to handle these for researchers in terms of ingress. The group discussed multiple options for this,
including internal mirrors of trusted sources, with data custodians choosing how open this access is, because reviewing
packages manually one by one does not keep up with genomics work.

Almost every analysis also reads public reference data, such as a reference genome and annotation databases. Most
pipelines download it on every run, which a TRE would block by default. The group discussed a few different approaches
to allow this, including just downloading reference files every time, or using a shared store. Downloading every time is
relatively cheap compared to the analysis, but needs an exception to the TRE's network controls, whereas shared stores
can be difficult to implement as workflows are sensitive to specific reference versions. Any genomics TRE would need to
account for this in some way, whether that's a general infrastructure problem or a TRE problem, and give custodians
enough levers to control this. The group decided that alongside workflow engines and software sources, this was distinct
enough to call-out in relation to a genomics TRE.

**What was decided.**

- Agreed: the requirements should call out software that changes the infrastructure, such as workflow engines and Spark
  clusters, although which line this sits under is still open. SATRE's reproducibility line ([2.1.11]) is unchanged.
- Agreed: a genomics TRE must solve access to reference data, with an open question on how, and at what level.
- Open: whether access to container registries should become Recommended, or stay Optional with a note.
- Open: a new requirement that the TRE provides a workflow engine, so that projects do not each run their own.

## Storage and data lifecycle

**What SATRE assumes.** SATRE expects the TRE to bring data into a project and keep it to the minimum ([3.1.13]). It
also expects the TRE to back data up ([2.6.01]), and to archive or delete it at the end of a project ([1.4.04],
[3.1.09]).

**What differs for genomics.** Genomics doesn't fundamentally conflict with the existing SATRE requirements, however at
genomics scale, each of these steps could be expensive. Objects can sometimes reach hundreds of gigabytes, with many
small files in-between, and if using cloud services, restoring data from archive tiers can take hours to days. The group
judged the genomics points on classification, ingress, egress and deletion to be notes about this scale.

The bigger difference is the model of who holds the data. The group discussed the following models:

- **Core datasets** exist before and after any TRE project, and the custodian holds them.
- **A TRE project** is a time-limited period of analysis. Its input is defined when it starts. It owns only its working
  data and derived data, and no data is kept when it ends.

Both of these have relevance to a genomics TRE, and this implies that the TRE is where data is used, not where it is
kept. There are possible exceptions, such as curated findings or variant classifications that may stay in the TRE as a
shared resource.

The model does not fix how data reaches a project. Views differed on whether reading data in place is essential to a
TRE, however at genomics scale, avoiding a copy in each project saves storage and reduces risk. At the end of a project,
there could be terabytes of data remaining which requires adequate deletion. Generally the group assumed that deletion
or withdrawal requests mid-project affect the next data release, not the current project, although this sits uneasily
with participants' right to have their data deleted.

All these factors represent a difference from other fields that simply copy data into their TRE in lower volumes, or
with lesser deletion requirements. Noting that health data could have similar participant requirements for consent or
deletion.

Additionally to this document, the review documents propose shared read-only storage, or API access such as GA4GH
htsget. [Costing a data-sharing platform](../costing-data-sharing-platform/costing-scrollytell/) models what different
storage choices cost.

**What was decided.**

- Agreed: the genomics points on the data lifecycle are notes about scale.
- Open: a TRE project as a time-limited view of datasets that the custodian holds, and how the requirements should state
  this model.
- Open: keeping curated findings in the TRE, the tools needed to carry out a deletion policy, and what happens to
  derived data at the end of a project.
- Proposed: a new requirement to avoid copies of large datasets, ingress from archive storage, API access to genomic
  data, and backups by type of data.

## Financial and cost management

**What SATRE assumes.** SATRE requires projects to know their costs and be able to pay them ([4.4.01]). It recommends
tracking costs for each project ([4.4.02]), and requires the TRE not to overspend ([2.3.04]). These lines suit a project
that rents a desktop for a known period.

**What differs for genomics.** Genomics costs depend on usage, which can be large, bursty and hard to estimate. A
misconfigured workflow can use a large share of a budget within hours, and misconfigured HPC clusters that are left on
can cost a lot. This represents a key difference in genomics from desktop-based TREs.

In addition to configuration, the custodian, the operator and the project may also be separate organisations, each
paying for different things. Other large-scale genomics platforms already handle usage-based costs. For example, the UK
Biobank platform blocks new work when an account passes its spending limit ([UKB-RAP
billing](https://dnanexus.gitbook.io/uk-biobank-rap/administrator/billing-and-costs)). This means that any TRE
definition should account for cost controls.

A [draft](../../requirements/satre-genomics/#4-4) of requirements for SATRE in this space covers cost attribution,
estimates, budget limits, and payment models, and represents the area where the group feels SATRE needs more. Estimates
may be inaccurate, and hard caps that stop jobs automatically were not favoured, but configurable budget limits are
likely a good addition to SATRE. These requirements aren't entirely unique to genomics, and are shared with fields like
AI too, although genomics feels them sharply.

**What was decided.**

- Agreed: genomics cost management needs more than notes, and will be written up as a separate section. Its focus is
  transparency, with an estimate before a job and the actual cost afterwards.
- Open: whether that section sets requirements, or describes approaches with their pros and cons.
- Open: budget limits with configurable action once hit, with Recommended favoured over Mandatory.
- Open: the wording on payment models and who pays.
- Proposed: raising cost tracking ([4.4.02]) to Mandatory.

## Egress and output checking

This topic was only partially discussed by the group, so a summary of current findings is presented.

**What SATRE requires.** SATRE requires an egress process ([3.1.05]) and disclosure control of outputs ([3.3]). It also
bans copying data out through the system clipboard ([2.1.01]).

**What was discussed.**

The group discussed preventing accidental leaks by trusted researchers as a TRE's core job, with views on deliberate
leaks differing. It was doubted whether a TRE could stop a determined attacker, and the focus of SATRE on clipboard
rules was argued as less relevant to genomics, which tends to lose data through bulk copying of files. The clipboard
rule may also present usability concerns for researchers. For now, the rule is kept, but flagged for a threat analysis
later. Despite this, controls such as blocking large downloads still give valuable protection, with organisations
deciding the balance between technical controls and signed agreements.

**What was decided.**

- Agreed: genomic egress is a note about scale.
- Open: the clipboard rule is kept, flagged for a threat analysis.
- Proposed: notes on checking genomic outputs.

## Open questions and next steps

**The rest of the review.** The group has not yet discussed the proposals on infrastructure, capacity, backups, access
control, output checking and data discovery. It still has to write up the cost section, and sort its list into needs
that are unique to genomics and needs shared with other fields.

**Where to start building.** Members' informal accounts of platforms they had used showed few that combine TRE controls
with genomics compute. Platforms had some required components of a genomics TRE, such as workflow management or scalable
compute, but lacked others, such as proper airlock controls, native workflow management or automatic scaling.

It was left open whether to start from a genomics platform and add TRE controls, or start from a TRE and add genomics
features.

**Choosing a platform.** Drawing on these findings, this report suggests checking five things first if choosing a
platform:

- dynamic compute that workflow engines and researchers can start and stop, with deletion afterwards;
- a workflow engine, with a supply of containers, software and reference data that does not need open internet access;
- access to custodian-held datasets without a full copy in each project, where data volumes make copies impractical;
- cost visibility and configurable limits that work with usage-based billing;
- an egress process that can handle large binary files and genomic outputs.

**Feeding back to SATRE.** SATRE's authors prefer to keep the specification general. The group plans to publish its
notes as the view of the Australian genomics community, and ask the SATRE team how they would like to receive them.

The working group will continue in 2027 as part of GUARDIANS Phase 2.
